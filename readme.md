# Beamforming with Neural Network Acceleration for Numerical Optimization of Frequency Transform Beamforming [with MATLAB Code]

Introduction  
Telecommunications and telecommunication systems play a critical role in today's society. Mobile wireless communication and data access networks are now available anywhere on the globe. The rapid development of global wireless systems has facilitated the transition from older generations to 4G+ and 5G networks. Currently, the sixth-generation (6G) system is under development to ensure high-speed, high-capacity, and high-quality communication requirements for the new millennium's social and industrial needs.  
However, even with the use of available advanced telecommunication technologies, wireless communication still suffers from interference caused by electromagnetic (EM) wave attenuation, scattering, and diffraction. This undesirable phenomenon occurs along the signal propagation path from the transmitter (TX) to the receiver (RX). From this perspective, it is clear that achieving the highest Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) between communication devices is necessary within the transmission path. One potential method to increase SNR is through active optimization of multipath wave propagation scenarios in dynamic environments to reduce path loss between TX and RX. In particular, intelligent beamforming is a promising solution that can ensure wireless connectivity between TX and RX in complex environments and achieve optimal SNR.  
Beamforming can be implemented using various antenna systems, including various configurations. In current base station transceivers (BTS) systems and mobile devices, MIMO (Multiple Input Multiple Output) antennas are used to achieve channel diversity [1-6]. Typically, such MIMO systems (or antenna arrays) can use basic antennas made from low-cost printed circuit board (PCB) technology, such as microstrip or coplanar, for sending and receiving various information [7-14].
There are several methods to create and manipulate beams in antenna arrays, ranging from the classic Butler matrix method to actively developed intelligent and reconfigurable antenna concepts. In 1961, Butler and Lowe proposed their passive beamforming method [15, 16]; this was a development of Blass's early work [17]. The Butler matrix is commonly used for feeding various antenna arrays with broad angle coverage [18]. Typical systems can include a N×N matrix of fixed phase shifters and hybrid couplers, where N is a power of 2. An antenna array equipped with such a matrix can have N input ports (beam ports) and N output ports (element ports), with power applied to N input ports and N antennas connected to N output ports. The Butler matrix provides power to elements through progressive phase differences between elements, directing the main beam of the antenna towards a selected direction. Beam direction can be controlled by switching input power between beam ports. It is possible to simultaneously activate one or even all N beams.
The Butterfly matrix can be implemented using microstrip lines, making it an affordable solution for achieving directional beamforming without the need for expensive active phase shifter arrays. Consequently, its manufacturing cost may only represent a small fraction of the cost of a mature antenna array feed network. However, its relative simplicity comes at the expense of having to deal with a massive structure and a fixed set of predefined beam directions. In fact, Figure 1 depicts a Butterfly-matrix-based beamforming system with eight input ports and eight antenna ports [18]. The system is capable of generating up to eight fixed beams, with their angles ranging from ±55°. For each antenna element, the design requires an input feed, a phase shifter, and a half-mix hybrid coupler. Typically, a 90° hybrid coupler is formed by four quarter-wavelength microstrip lines. Therefore, at a 5GHz operating frequency, each coupler occupies about 1cm² of area. Additionally, additional phase compensation lines are required to achieve the necessary phase shift within the Butler matrix [18]. As such, integrating the Butler network with the antenna array elements on the same PCB may impose limitations on the density of component placement, especially when the number of input ports approaches the number of components.
In the context of more expensive active control antenna systems, there are various types of electronic/digital control phased array antennas that can be assembled in linear, planar, or conformal configurations [19-23]. Such antennas can provide flexible electronic beam steering in less than milliseconds, eliminating the need for mechanical steering. They can produce multiple beams simultaneously and perform multifunctional operations through independent beam control. Unlike passive phased array antennas, active phased array antennas can also concurrently transmit and receive multiple radio signals at multiple frequencies in different directions, used in radar applications for surveillance and tracking. Although highly reconfigurable, these devices may also suffer from limitations such as limited coverage in both azimuth and elevation planes (for linear arrays, beam steering is only possible in a single plane). Moreover, active phased array networks are very complex and require components with complex electronic controls, which can be costly to obtain despite their frequency hopping capabilities being limited [24-26].
Over the past decade, an alternative concept has emerged that combines the advantages of passive systems (e.g., low energy consumption and cost) with active systems (e.g., flexible reconfiguration): the concept of programmable metasurfaces (PMS) [27-29]. In recent years, presbyopia has been actively investigated. Specifically, it has been demonstrated that PMS or reflective intelligent surfaces (RIS) can be used to adjust channel propagation conditions [30,31]. In PMS enhanced systems, the radiation patterns of TX and RX antennas can be dynamically controlled to facilitate wireless communication in changing propagation environments.
In practice, it is known that microwave or optical metasurfaces (MS) can alter the propagation direction of EM waves by generating phase gradients along their surface [32, 33]. Specifically, MS can be used as mirrors in reconfigurable reflector antennas. In such antennas, by controlling the phase and amplitude of the wavefront reflected from the MS, universal beamforming can be achieved [34-37]. However, to freely control the reflection amplitude at the MS lattice site, active components (such as amplifiers or impedance inverters) may be necessary. In this case, the effective surface impedance of the MS becomes complex. The sign of the real part of the surface impedance determines whether the signal reflected locally at that point is attenuated or amplified. On the other hand, if reflection amplitude control is not required, the MS can be completely passive (resistive). Even in purely resistive MSs, some form of amplitude control can be implemented through what can be called "surface mode engineering," where the surface modes supported by the MS are tuned to reallocate reactive power (i.e., local oscillator power).
When comparing systems based on PMS with those based on Butler matrices, it can be noted that for each unit cell, the system based on PMS typically only requires a few surface mount (SMT) components with dimensions in the range of 1 mm or smaller, whereas the Butler matrix requires a hybrid coupler and phase shifter network realized with microstrip lines, whose dimensions are dictated by the operating wavelength and power handling requirements. Therefore, in the system based on PMS, the size of the unit cell is not constrained by the size of the auxiliary devices (i.e., controllable components). At microwave frequencies, this allows for significant reduction in the size of the unit cell compared to the wavelength (e.g., from one-tenth to one-fifth of the wavelength), which is important for complex beamforming scenarios as it improves the accuracy of the field representation within the aperture.
For example, a single-layer passive MS is proposed in [38, 39] to control the phase and amplitude of the EM field in the antenna aperture plane. MS aims to convert the cylindrical incident wave into a directional far-field pattern with a main beam tilted by 30° relative to the MS normal. A fully-reactive MS has been generated using an optimized algorithm to produce the required near and far fields. In their method, the MS surface impedance is initially obtained as a function of position from the desired aperture field distribution. However, the impedance found in this way is complex, where the real part changes sign at different positions on the MS. Next, by applying global optimization to the surface impedance distribution, an MS with only reactance is obtained while maintaining the same far-field radiation pattern, allowing the real part to be neglected. In summary, such optimization adjustments the MS surface impedance at different locations to generate an appropriate evanescent wave spectrum near the MS and maintain the redistribution of the reactive power on the MS and produce the desired far-field pattern.

Conclusion
An inverse design method is introduced, which integrates FT-based beamforming, numerical optimization, and machine learning techniques. In our approach, firstly, the desired far-field radiation pattern is obtained by applying a Fourier transform to the required aperture field distribution. Subsequently, the amplitude information of the aperture field is discarded; however, the phase information is retained and used as input for the developed optimization algorithm, further enhancing the accuracy of beamforming. Next, this optimized FT-based beamforming method is employed to generate datasets for the desired aperture phase distribution with multiple single-beam and dual-beam scenarios. The generated datasets are then used to train a Beamforming Feedforward Neural Network (BFNN), which can generate the necessary phase distribution data within fractions of a second.
Additionally, to provide control for the PMS and generate the necessary control voltages for the PMS's unit cells, we propose using another training control neural network (CTRLNN), which can also perform additional functions in practical applications, such as compensating for the coupling of unit cells in the PMS. Real-time calculation of the bias voltage required for controlling the unit cells of the PMS is performed. The BFNN and CTRLNN networks have been trained on the generated dataset for many beamforming scenarios. The performance of the neural networks has been validated. In the best case, the correlation between the output of the BFNN and the results of an FT-based optimization beamforming method is up to 98%, and it is generally above 90% in other cases. The verified performance of the CTRLNN is also very high, with a control voltage MSE below 10^-4 V2.
Overall, we have observed that when reproducing the required radiation pattern, whether optimized FT-based methods or trained artificial neural networks are used, despite ignoring the amplitude information at the Fourier transform stage, there is a high precision. However, in high incidence angles, parasitic sidelobes may appear in the reconstructed pattern, a phenomenon related to the number of unit cells along and across rows and columns studied in PMS samples.
